Context

This is a small project — the account creation flow for a single digital publication. The reason it's here is that it's a useful window into how I approach design problems: by mapping the problem space carefully before touching any UI.

Waiting Magazine launched a digital edition alongside the print publication. The model was metered access: readers could view a limited number of articles for free before being asked to subscribe. Memberstack was the chosen platform for membership and access management.

The scope was deliberately narrow: design the experience around that paywall moment. Not the digital edition's full site design, not the content itself — just the flow from "you've hit the limit" to "you're a subscriber." It's a short path, but the decisions in it matter.

Problem

Metered access UX is deceptively complex. The reader can be in many different states depending on what they've done before: anonymous and within the free limit, anonymous and at the limit, has an account but no active subscription, has an active subscription, or has a lapsed subscription. Each state calls for different UI treatment and different copy.

The paywall moment itself is a high-stakes interaction. A reader who hits a paywall is by definition engaged — they've read enough to want more. That's a good moment to convert. But paywalls are also interruptions, and interruptions create friction. The design question was: how do you make the transition from "free reader" to "subscriber" feel like a natural continuation of the reading experience rather than a hard stop?

The technical constraint was Memberstack's platform: powerful and well-documented, but with default flows that are generic and wouldn't feel consistent with a design publication's aesthetic and voice. Everything visible to the reader needed custom design.

Approach

The first step was a complete state map — every situation a reader could be in when they encountered the paywall or account system, and what should happen in each case. This sounds obvious, but it's easy to design for the happy path (anonymous reader, hits paywall, subscribes, reads on) while leaving edge cases as afterthoughts. Lapsed subscribers, in particular, often get awkward treatment: the system recognizes them as existing users but the UI treats them like strangers.

For each state, I worked through three questions: What does the reader know about their situation right now? What do they need in order to take the next step? What's the minimum friction path to get them there? Those questions made the copy and UI decisions much more concrete.

On the persuasion side: I wanted the paywall to feel like an invitation from the publication rather than a gate. That's mostly a copy problem. "Subscribe to keep reading" is a transaction; "Waiting is reader-supported — join us" is a different relationship. The copy should reflect what the publication actually is.

The state map came first — a diagram showing every reader state, the conditions that trigger a state change, and the UI component that should appear in each case. Having this on paper before opening any design tool meant that decisions about the UI were always traceable back to a specific user situation rather than an abstract aesthetic preference.

Wireframes followed, roughly in order of traffic: the paywall modal first (most people see this), then the sign-up flow, then the sign-in flow for returning users, then the account page, then the lapsed-subscription state. Memberstack handles the authentication logic; the design work was the wrapper around it — what does the reader see, and what are they being asked to do?

Copy was written alongside the UI, not after. The copy decisions — what to say at the paywall, how to describe the subscription tiers, what confirmation language to use — are design decisions. Leaving them as Lorem Ipsum until the visual design is "done" produces designs that look right but don't work.

Implementation in Memberstack involved configuring the membership tiers, article gating logic, and custom UI overrides. The gating logic — counting articles read per session, per user, resetting counts correctly — required careful testing across the different state combinations, including logged-out users in incognito browsers and users who cleared their cookies.

Outcome
Final implementation — live screen

Subscription flow, live on waitingmag.com

The system shipped and handles all the user states correctly — including the edge cases. The paywall experience feels consistent with the rest of the digital edition: same typography, same tone, same aesthetic register. It doesn't feel like you've suddenly left the publication and ended up on a SaaS signup page.

The thing I'd do differently: involve someone outside the project in usability testing earlier. I knew the flow too well by the time I was testing it, which made it easy to miss confusion points that were obvious to a first-time reader. For a flow with this much riding on it — someone who's ready to subscribe but could be lost to friction — that's worth investing in.

Good paywall design isn't really about the paywall. It's about making the reader's relationship with the publication feel continuous — before, during, and after the subscription decision.
